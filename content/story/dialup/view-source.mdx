<Glance>
In 1999 I learned the web the way everyone did: click View Source, copy the clever bits, figure out why they worked. The site I studied most was Spiremedia's. When a flyer at school said they were hiring a receptionist, I emailed a link to my Flash portfolio under the subject line "I don't want to be your receptionist." My title was Production Artist. For the first six months of my career, code and design were the same thing.
</Glance>

I'd been poring over Spiremedia's portfolio for months, asking the questions you ask when you're learning by looking. What about this is applicable to my own work? What can I discard? What can I change to make it mine, my clients' own? How did they code that? Their own site was as considered as their client work. I'd click View Source, copy out some of their HTML, and learn from their ideas. I never dreamed I'd work there. I was a student with ten months of contract work.

Then, in the cramped first-floor hall of the 300 building at the Art Institute of Colorado, a flyer in Franklin Gothic Condensed: SPIREMEDIA IS HIRING A RECEPTIONIST. To me it was etched in gold. The privilege of my education leapfrogged me past applying for the job they were actually hiring for. The reply asked for an interview.

![Spiremedia's building in 1999. Photo by Flickr user elmada.](/writing/why-im-a-designer-part-2-view-source/3063789530_9128883c59_b.webp)

The building was past Lower Downtown, on a street no one I knew had heard of, across a dirt lot I recognized from high school. Inside: five thousand square feet of exactly what I'd been looking for since junior high. No drywall, no drop ceilings, no neckties. Dogs. Nobody over thirty. Thick wooden columns from long before the offices were built around them, covered in artwork and ethernet cables. The waiting area was two old wooden school chairs facing a pinball machine and Asteroids. The music was loud and the man interviewing me had about a thousand toys on his desk. I was home again.

My first two clients were dot-coms. LeagueLink let recreational sports leagues organize dues, messages and schedules; its only JavaScript was image rollovers. Click, page load. Click, page load. Ajax would be a cleaning supply for another five or six years. Why.com was a search engine where users rated the results, with a question mark for the dot in the logo. It was the first time anyone in our office had heard of Google, which powered the search. We thought it was a weird choice.

![Why.com as it launched: a user-rated guide to the web, 2000.](/writing/why-im-a-designer-part-2-view-source/screen-shot-2015-07-08-at-12-17-58-pm-1024x783.webp)

**Scott Upton**, my lead on both projects, turned out to be the designer whose work I'd admired most from afar. He was doing the work himself, and his structural decisions cascaded down to my screens, so whatever style I'd brought from school went out the window; my job was to extend someone else's partly defined system. There were no Photoshop files. He was building a clickable prototype, a shadow of the app the client's developers were building, so we could use the site as we designed it and tell when an idea wasn't working long before anyone else saw it. And he was patient, and always on the user's side. He could sketch in front of a client and win them to a better idea than the one they'd brought. Clean lines and clear ideas won, most of the time.

<Inhabit>
What sucked about the web in 1999: fonts, as long as they were Verdana, Arial, Trebuchet, Georgia or Times. 56k modems at home. Colors darker on Windows, every time, on monitors we would never calibrate. No standards until Zeldman and the Web Standards Project got the browser vendors talking. No prior art for something as basic as navigation.

What was awesome about it: all of that. Without a mode of operation to follow, we got to make things up. We weren't desktop designers, because we had no low-level control and the page metaphor broke the application anyway. We weren't graphic designers, because people had to use what we made. We weren't multimedia designers, because our screens were a document markup language with a page load between every click. We weren't developers, because they used ColdFusion and SQL and acronyms I didn't understand. We improvised and changed course fast, which was and is the whole benefit of the platform.

For the first six months of my professional career, code and design were one and the same. No developer alive would touch Flash, and having our hands in the code gave us a control over the result that no wireframe or mockup could. The client saw screenshots, but the work underneath had a web-native method baked in: we knew the screens could be built because we were already building them.

Scott taught me to design in the material. Everything since, [the agents included](#piece-chapter-this-site), is that lesson again.
</Inhabit>
