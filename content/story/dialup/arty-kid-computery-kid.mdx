<Glance>
I was an "arty kid" and a "computery kid," and for a long time nobody told me those were different things. For junior high I was bussed across San Diego to a computer-math-science magnet school, where I took my first coding class at 12, in 1991. We coded a windmill in BASIC, a Myst-like game in HyperCard, and a firewood calculator in Pascal. The only part I was good at was the part that happened on the screen.
</Glance>

Art came first. My first art class was at the neighborhood rec center when I was seven, in the building where my brother played basketball. Art taught me how to make a space in my head to play with a visual idea, how to get it onto paper, and how to learn from a failed experiment on a fresh sheet. I used the backs of worksheets for this instead of the fronts, to several teachers' chagrin.

Computers came right after. Nobody told me I was a computery kid because there weren't computers around to be computery with until I was about seven. But by third grade, when our classroom got an Apple ][ that none of the adults could hook up, I could look at the wires and see where each one went. When the Macintosh arrived, my cousins and I spent hours in MacPaint printing ten-page birthday banners on the perforated paper. I loved computers as a kid for one reason: they were clearly tools for making art.

![Bell Junior High School, San Diego, 1993. Taking Instagrams before it was cool.](/writing/why-im-a-designer-part-1-a-timeline/3374251552_86f74fa20f_o.webp)

**Bell Junior High, 1991 to 1994.** A computer-math-science magnet school that bussed kids in from all over San Diego, with a programming class every year alongside the math and science. I took my first coding class at a public school at 12, which I couldn't appreciate the rarity of then and can now. There weren't enough machines, so we coded in pairs.

Seventh grade: a frame-by-frame animation in BASIC, placing each frame's pixels on an 80×80 grid, wiping it, drawing the next. My partner Quentin and I made a two-frame windmill that looked like it was spinning when it looped. Animated GIFs before there were browsers to show them in.

Eighth grade: we learned about scanners, and my partner Kenny and I used HyperCard to build an interactive, Myst-like game. That was my first onMouseUp. I had no idea how many thousand times I'd write it again.

Freshman year it got harder. Pascal forms that took a user's input, ran it through functions and put the answer back on the screen. We computed how many cords of firewood you'd get from a theoretical tree, and I focused, again, on how the data looked when it was displayed. My partner Lauren understood the math; without her I doubt any of my projects would have compiled. When I learned ActionScript and JavaScript years later, I could still hear her patiently explaining closing curly brackets.

<Inhabit>
What I learned that semester, at Lauren's expense, was that when the code strayed too far from a visual result, there were people who took to that kind of work better than I did. My part was when something had to happen on the screen. That's still my part.

And I learned what I'd spend my whole career looking for, without the words for it yet: a smart, odd, collaborative group of people in a room, making digital art together. I found it again [in a warehouse in Denver in 1999](#piece-chapter-view-source), and in a distributed company a decade after that.

I can't overstate what a few teachers and the machines in a few relatives' houses did for me. I don't know who I'd be without them.
</Inhabit>
