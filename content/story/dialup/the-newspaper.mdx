<Glance>
When we moved to Colorado, the gap between my art and my computers widened for two years. The better-equipped labs at my new school were for spreadsheets and Word documents. Senior year I joined the newspaper staff as graphic artist and music columnist, got the one big Mac in the room, and we put out the first high school paper in Colorado made entirely on computers.
</Glance>

Sophomore and junior year in a suburban Colorado high school were a technical and cultural dark age. The labs were full of machines nobody used for anything creative. The school was not kind to weird kids, and I was one. So I drew, I wrote angsty poetry, I played guitar, and I got better at all three, which is how I learned that art could be a place to go. As long as I was making something that was improving, I could handle whatever got said in the hallway.

Senior year turned. I'd found the other outcasts, and with that borrowed confidence I joined the newspaper as an elective, which put me in front of a computer that expected me to make something again. The writers worked on the nine-inch black-and-white Classics along the wall. I was allowed the PowerMac 5200 LC with the scanner attached and Photoshop 3.0 installed. My music column was called Cognitive Dissonance. I'm not making that up.

That year was the first time a high school newspaper in Colorado was produced one hundred percent digitally. Every other school was still mocking up pages with printouts, glue and tape and sending camera-ready art to the printer. We sent files.

I felt at home again. I made graphics for the paper during class and stayed after to scan my drawings and work on them. On weekends I took photos downtown, scanned the prints, and blended them into strange images to print and hang in my locker. I still didn't know what the words "graphic design" meant. When my newspaper teacher suggested an independent study in it for the spring semester, overseen by her and the head of the art department, I took it. I could investigate what graphic design was, make things nobody had assigned, and get ready for art school.

<Inhabit>
Two things from those years are still with me. One is that the tools matter less than whether anyone lets you use them for making. A room full of better computers did nothing for me for two years; one Performa with a scanner changed the direction of my life. The other is the teachers. A newspaper adviser and an art director who let a kid define his own course of study, in 1997, in a suburban public school. I can't imagine who or where I'd be without them.
</Inhabit>
