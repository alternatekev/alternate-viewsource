<Glance>
I worked through art school doing digital pre-press three blocks from campus, pre-flighting other students' QuarkXPress files off Zip disks and staying after my shift to print my own work at a discount. Ten to twelve hours a day of design. Then I lost that job on Halloween 1998, and between then and January 1999 I taught myself HTML (ok, GoLive CyberStudio helped).
</Glance>

Pre-press is where you find out how much can go wrong between a designer's file and a finished piece of paper. The amount of work it took to get a QuarkXPress document from someone's Zip disk to print correctly on our digital presses was obscene, and the details that broke it were the kind a customer could easily miss and never know until the box arrived. As school went on, the classes leaned harder on getting offset prints produced, an expensive craft I had zero interest in learning.

The web was the obvious way out. It put me back behind the scenes on the computer, writing the code for the interface itself instead of using someone else's interface to make art. I'd been introduced to that in HyperCard six years earlier and I hadn't forgotten it.

It was also a strange choice for a designer in 1998. I was giving up fine typographic control, throwing faithful color out the window, and choosing lower resolution over higher. I did it willfully and aggressively, because I could make something, and make it fast, and people could see it anywhere in the world in seconds. Why on earth would I care about printing things?

Halloween 1998: I lost the pre-press job and found myself without work for a few months, and I did the thing that felt natural. I taught myself HTML. GoLive CyberStudio helped more than I admitted at the time.

January 1999: my first web design job, hourly, at a small shop called The Internet Design Firm. I was still in school. In my first week there I AltaVista'd "Denver web design companies" (we didn't Google back then) to see what was happening in town, and one portfolio redefined for me what a web site could be. That story is [the next chapter](#piece-chapter-view-source).

<Inhabit>
The pre-press job taught me something I didn't have words for until much later: the gap between the design and the thing is where the work dies. In print, that gap was a Zip disk and a RIP. On the web, it was a developer building an approximation of a Photoshop file. The whole rest of this site is me trying to close that gap, one medium at a time, by learning the material well enough to make the thing myself.

I've never once held a job whose title was developer, and every job I've ever had was easier to get because I could code. I wrote that in 2010. It's truer now.
</Inhabit>
