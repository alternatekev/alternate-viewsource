<Glance>
May 2001: prizes in the5k, the contest for pages under five kilobytes. 2001 to 2005: unemployed, lots of freelance, and a custom content management system in PHP every time I launched a site for a client. June 2005: alternate.org moved to WordPress 1.5 within months of its release. September 2005: I was 26, and about to spend four years at a company in Boulder called Wall Street on Demand.
</Glance>

I spent 2001 to 2005 doing the following. Being unemployed. Lots of freelance. I built a web page with only 5 kilobytes of code. Building custom content management systems in PHP every time I launched a website for a client. Getting a job where I helped Einstein sell bagels on the internet. And all this time, I blogged. I blogged when Napster was shut down, I blogged when the Twin Towers fell, I blogged when Apple launched the iPod, on a system built in PHP3 that a friend and I paid $500 for, then on a custom system I wrote in PHP 4, then on WordPress.

Client work was now very serious, and playing with new ideas often wasn't the best choice for those projects. The blog was where I kept my code chops up, and it was the one site with an audience I could call my own. Ego and empathy. I contend you need both.

In September 2005, I was 26 years old. I called Scott Upton on the phone and asked if he was happy at his job and if they were hiring. A week later I'd had my interviews, handed in my resignation at Spiremedia for a second time, and was about to spend four years at Wall Street on Demand.

Wall Street on Demand took hundreds of live-streaming stock market data feeds and turned them into interfaces for making financial decisions, for everyone from the home investor managing a retirement account to the institutional investor trading hundreds of millions of dollars at a time. If I put a percentage instead of a dollar amount in a table cell showing Earnings Per Share, it would be called out long before my work made it to the client's inbox. It felt like being in college again. A little fish in a big pond, and it was good for me. I filled book after book with notes. (Grid-lined Moleskines. Perfect for drawing charts.)

Every Friday morning the design staff gathered, coffee in hand, in a room named after Charles and Ray Eames, and nerded out: what was near or just launched, so the rest of us could learn from the decisions; URLs we'd saved up all week, critiqued as a group with no one in the room defending them; and what about our own process wasn't working.

<Inhabit>
I resisted the collaboration at first. My creative director checked in on my progress multiple times a day. He'd show up right behind me at my desk and pull up a chair. "Whatcha up to, Kev?" I'd tab back over to Photoshop and show him my work. "Why'd you use yellow here? It's not in the palette." This didn't last long, and I learned an incredible amount of data visualization and financial knowledge directly from him.

The moment of truth was when work started getting really hard. I was the only designer on a project for the first time, driving home to Denver every night in a daze. Then an email from him, in its entirety: "Kevin, I need to you to engage now." I had no idea what it meant. I was afraid at first. Then I decided it was time to start using my design skills to actually get design accomplished. I drew diagrams of how I understood the system to work, took them to the client and then to our developers, and iterated until everyone agreed. Then I started wireframing in HTML. We had clickable prototypes with no CSS before we had a design language. By the time it launched, I'd written the final production HTML and handed it to our developers, who plugged in the data calls.

What got us ahead was that the whole team came together, not that I could do the design work perfectly in a silo on my own, which was what I'd thought my role as a designer was. At the end of that project I had a sense of pride I'd never felt before.

</Inhabit>
