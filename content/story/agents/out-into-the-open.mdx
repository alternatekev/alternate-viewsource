<Glance>
March 2026: the team is told it's turning entirely to agentic development. I told them the truth and invited discussion. The skills people had been building privately came out at all-hands, got shaped in 1:1s, went into the repo, then into the MCP for contributing developers on other teams. We measure CI time, test memory, page load, asset delivery and who tags their PRs. We measure many things.
</Glance>

I told them the truth, and invited discussion. The room's consensus came back in one line: "We're already doing this, basically." That was true, and it was the whole announcement. We talked a lot about what makes a person special to the process, and how to bring more of that, not less.

We'd all been working on agentic workflows on our own already, quietly. So the first thing I did was make room for it: dedicated time in all-hands for developers to bring what they'd built out into the open. Once that was a cadence, I started shaping it in 1:1s. More detailed questions. Broaden this one. Tighten that one. Try the alternative and tell me what happened. When everyone had private skills they actually used, we standardized them and brought them into the repo for the whole team. Then they went into the MCP, so a developer on another team contributing to the library could find them without asking us.

That's the sequence, and I'd run it again anywhere: private, then shown, then shaped, then shared, then discoverable. The order matters. Standardize too early and you get the tool nobody uses. Too late and you have nine versions of the same skill.

**What we measure.** CI time and test memory. Page-load time and asset-delivery time. Who tags their pull requests as AI-assisted. That last one taught us something: every PR was AI-assisted, and not all of them were tagged, because not everyone was using the tools that carried the tag. We added the tagging to our own skills, and the count got honest. The same rule holds for anything that answers in the team's name: approve, edit or skip is the right loop for a bot that speaks for a team, and it never posts on its own.

<Inhabit>
**A week, across three teams.** The design-system team triages bugs, builds new features onto old components, migrates the library to a new stack on a new platform, brings in partner teams to contribute the things they need the system to do, meets with the UX designers for the latest and gives feedback, and works with the other two teams to get the newest version into the product. The modernization team sets standards for micro-apps and single-page apps to raise customer scores, takes small bites out of page-load time, and builds the skills that help other teams modernize their pages, the API side and the UI side, so they're ready to become single-page apps. The platform team owns the tooling, and this quarter that meant moving the runtime up several major versions with every upgrade that came with it: the bundler, the build and test tools. One test suite went from eleven minutes to four. Another from eight minutes to forty seconds. Across every pipeline the team computed it at thirteen weeks of CI time saved, every week.

The support rotation for the design system, which used to be a week of one engineer's time answering the channel, is now two jobs: cutting versions and approving the PRs that consuming teams open against the library. Everything else is answered from the same documentation the agents read.

People ask about pushback. There wasn't any on my teams. We'd been integrating agents at our own discretion for a while, and I'd been pushing to accelerate, so when it became required it was just one more step: stop touching the code by hand.

I have an agent draft my Monday update to leadership from the week's evidence. It turned out the draft was for me. At the end of the quarter, when I sit down to plan, it's the record of what actually happened, week by week, instead of what I remember happening.
</Inhabit>
