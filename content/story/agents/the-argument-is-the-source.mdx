<Glance>
Slides are where talks go to die. In September 2026 I built a tool with an agent in which a talk is one piece of work with two layers, its structure and its presentation, and I can look at it from either angle: as an outline, premise to arc to beats to sequence, or as the live deck, which exports to PDF, PowerPoint or Keynote. The first talk through it is about my three teams, and the metaphor is a superconductor.
</Glance>

For a few years I gave the monthly release talk to a couple hundred engineers, and a longer one when the teams needed a story. In 2023, a talk on visual flow to the developers building with the system. In 2024, a series teaching React to designers and developers who didn't think they were React people. The people in those rooms are the ones the design system is for. I found out how those talks had landed from the private messages afterward, from people I didn't know.

The way I'd made the decks for twenty years was the way everyone does: open the slide app, make a title, lose the argument by slide nine.

The fix was to stop treating the slides as a separate thing. A talk is one piece of work. Looked at from one angle it's structure: the premise, the one sentence the talk exists to make true; then the arc, then the beats, each with a stable id, then the sequence. Looked at from the other it's presentation: the slides themselves, live in the browser. Both views edit the same talk. I reorder and rewrite in either, gather images with their credits, and the other view is already current.

The presentation layer carries my rules, written down: one idea per slide, the second line is the loud one, real photographs and real interfaces, never decorative illustration, no gradients, mostly empty slides, Merlin Mann's pacing. It lives as HTML, because the browser is where I design, and exports to PDF, PowerPoint and Keynote because the room doesn't care where I design.

<Inhabit>
The talk in progress as I write this is about the design system's teams, and the metaphor is a superconductor: resistance drops to zero below a critical temperature, and current flows without loss. The premise underneath it is the one I've been giving since 2001, when I built a web page in five kilobytes: performance is experience. Speed is not a feature of the thing. It's what the thing feels like.

Nobody writes their own presentation software. It's a solved problem with a thousand products and all of them start from the slide. Describing the system I actually wanted, where structure and slides are the same talk seen two ways, took a weekend with an agent, because the agent could write the views, the exporters and the Keynote adapter while I argued about the beats. (The Keynote adapter is still arguing back.)

The tool you build for yourself encodes what you believe. This one believes in a unified design process: structure and presentation are layers of one piece of work, viewed from different angles. Now it's a skill file.
</Inhabit>
